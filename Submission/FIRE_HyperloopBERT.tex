\title{Same Models, Opposite Conclusions: Testing the Robustness of
       Architecture Bias Comparisons}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------- abstract
\begin{abstract}
Pre-trained language encoders are reused across retrieval, ranking and
classification systems. A stereotype that an encoder learns in pre-training can
therefore reach every system built on that encoder. Some recent encoders reuse
one transformer block at several depths to save memory. A lower stereotype score
for such a design would bear on the choice of encoder. This study tests whether
such a score difference belongs to the design. Four encoders that differ only in
block reuse were trained from scratch on the same text. The encoders were
compared at checkpoints of equal validation loss on two stereotype benchmarks.
At the most-trained common checkpoint, two block-reusing encoders scored about
one third lower than the unshared encoder. Closer testing did not support
crediting that gap to block reuse. The gap reversed at less-trained checkpoints
and was absent under the benchmark's official scoring rule. A second training
run of two encoders did not reproduce the gap. Two runs of one design differed
nearly as much as the two designs did. The study covers four encoders, two
benchmarks, three scoring rules and seven training levels. A bias comparison
between architectures therefore needs several training levels, several training
runs and more than one scoring rule.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Retrieval and classification systems are often built on a pre-trained language
encoder. One encoder is pre-trained once and then reused, usually after
fine-tuning, in rankers, classifiers and content filters. A stereotype
association learned during pre-training can therefore reach each of those
components. In this paper, a stereotype association refers to a higher model
score for text that links a social group with a common stereotype. Developers
who choose between encoders have reason to prefer a design that carries less of
this association.

Stereotype benchmarks measure the association in masked language models, which
are encoders trained to predict hidden words from their context.
\emph{CrowS-Pairs} and \emph{StereoSet} give the encoder paired texts that differ
only in the social group or in the completion
\citep{nangia2020crows,nadeem2021stereoset}. One \emph{CrowS-Pairs} pair
contrasts ``\emph{Cheryl} was bad at saving money and thus had a financial
crisis'' with the same sentence about \emph{John}. Each sentence is scored by how
well the encoder predicts its words. An encoder that scores the stereotypical
member higher across many pairs records a positive stereotype effect.

Some recent encoder designs reuse the same transformer block at several depths.
Reuse lowers the number of stored parameters, and looped designs apply a shared
group of blocks several times in a row
\citep{lan2020albert,saunshi2025looped,zeitoun2026hyperloop}. The studies cited
here report language quality, efficiency and reasoning accuracy. They do not
measure stereotype association. Released checkpoints cannot answer the question
either, because they differ in training text, tokeniser and training length. A
model that reuses blocks also learns less from the same text. A difference
measured after equal training can therefore reflect training progress rather
than design.

This study tests whether a lower stereotype effect can be credited to block
reuse once training progress is held level. Four encoders that differ only in
block reuse were trained from scratch on the same text, in the same order and
with the same tokeniser. Validation loss, the average prediction error on
held-out text, measures how far each encoder has trained. The encoders were
compared only at matched points, which are checkpoints where every encoder
reaches the same validation loss. The comparison was repeated at seven loss
levels, under three scoring rules and on a second benchmark. Two encoders were
also trained a second time from a different random initialisation. Two
capability checks test whether the encoders follow the sentences they score: a
pronoun-resolution test and fine-tuning on sentence classification.

This paper makes three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The study trained four encoders that differ only in block reuse on
      identical text and compared them at seven levels of validation loss. A
      stereotype difference between architectures can then be separated from
      a difference in training progress. Comparisons between released
      checkpoints cannot make that separation.
\item At the most-trained matched point, two block-reusing encoders scored
      significantly lower than the unshared encoder on \emph{CrowS-Pairs}. That
      gap reversed at less-trained matched points and did not survive an
      adjustment for the remaining loss differences. Neither the benchmark's
      official scoring rule nor a second training run reproduced the gap. In
      these four encoders, a single matched comparison did not show that block
      reuse lowers stereotype association.
\item Zero-shot pronoun resolution stayed near chance for every encoder and for a
      public BERT model. After fine-tuning, the same encoders classified
      sentence sentiment with similar accuracy. Stereotype scores of encoders at
      this scale describe word association. Evidence of sentence understanding
      has to come from fine-tuned tasks.
\end{enumerate}

% ================================================================ related work
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

A claim that one encoder design carries less stereotype association joins two
lines of work that rarely meet. Bias benchmarks study how to measure the
association but hold the architecture fixed. Weight-reuse studies change the
architecture but measure quality and cost. Capacity links the two lines, because
a smaller model learns less from the same text and a less-trained model can score
differently on a benchmark.

Masked-position probing scores the words a masked language model predicts at
hidden positions to measure a social association
\citep{kurita2019measuring}. \emph{CrowS-Pairs} and \emph{StereoSet} build on
this idea with paired stereotypical and anti-stereotypical texts
\citep{nangia2020crows,nadeem2021stereoset}. \citet{blodgett2021salmon} found
unclear or inconsistent items in both benchmarks. \citet{wang2025difference}
separate association benchmarks from tests of unequal treatment, and place both
benchmarks in the association group. These studies examine whether a benchmark
measures what it claims. They leave open whether a comparison between two
models stays the same when the scoring rule or the training level changes.

Weight reuse across depth is an established way to save parameters. ALBERT ties
one encoder block across all layers to reduce storage \citep{lan2020albert}.
\citet{saunshi2025looped} show that a block repeated in a loop can approach the
reasoning performance of a deeper unshared model. \citet{bae2025mor} learn a
per-token recursion depth over a shared block, and related recurrent-depth
models scale the same idea \citep{geiping2025huginn,zhu2025ouro,frey2026adaptive}.
A residual connection adds a block's input to its output.
Hyper-connections widen that connection into several parallel streams with
learned mixing \citep{zhu2025hyperconnections}, and \citet{xie2025mhc} constrain
the mixing for stability. \citet{zeitoun2026hyperloop} combine looping with
hyper-connections. These studies report language quality, throughput, parameter
efficiency and reasoning accuracy. Social-bias measurements for these designs are
not part of them.

Compression studies are the closest precedent for a link between capacity and
measured bias. Pruning and other compression methods can change fairness
measurements \citep{hooker2020characterising,xu2022compression,ramesh2023comparative},
and \citet{voria2026tracing} localise stereotype-carrying neurons inside
pre-trained transformers. In these studies the model is changed after training,
so the compressed and the original model have seen the same text for the same
time. A design that reuses weights from the start instead reaches each level of
quality after a different amount of training.

Fairness in information access is an established concern for retrieval and
ranking \citep{ekstrand2022fairness,singh2018fairness}. An encoder-level bias
difference would bear on model selection for such systems. Three gaps
follow from the work reviewed. Weight-reusing encoders have no stereotype
measurements. Model comparisons on association benchmarks are rarely repeated
across scoring rules. Comparisons at one training point cannot separate design
from training progress. This study closes the first gap by measuring four
weight-reusing and unshared encoders trained on identical text. It addresses the
second and the third by repeating each comparison under three scoring rules and
at seven matched levels of validation loss.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

One corpus trains the encoders, and three public datasets measure stereotype
association or sentence understanding, with examples in Table~\ref{tab:data}.

All encoders are pre-trained on \emph{FineWeb-Edu}, a filtered subset of web text
selected for educational content \citep{penedo2024fineweb}. Documents are read in
one fixed order at a sequence length of 128 tokens, and all encoders share one
tokeniser. No difference between encoders can therefore come from the training
text or from how it is split into tokens.

The two stereotype benchmarks are used without fine-tuning. \emph{CrowS-Pairs}
holds 1508 sentence pairs in nine bias categories \citep{nangia2020crows}. The
field \texttt{sent\_more} holds the more stereotypical member of each pair and
\texttt{sent\_less} the other member. A direction label marks whether
\texttt{sent\_more} states a stereotype about a disadvantaged group
(\texttt{stereo}) or \texttt{sent\_less} violates one (\texttt{antistereo}).
Every rule in this study scores each pair as \texttt{sent\_more} against
\texttt{sent\_less}, as the benchmark's released scorer does. \emph{StereoSet} is
used in its intra-sentence split of 2106 items across four bias types
\citep{nadeem2021stereoset}. Each item gives a context sentence with a blank and
three candidate fillers: stereotypical, anti-stereotypical and unrelated.

\emph{WinoBias} tests pronoun resolution in sentences that name two occupations
\citep{zhao2018winobias}. A pronoun in each sentence refers to one of the two
occupations. The pro-stereotypical and anti-stereotypical versions of an item
differ only in the gender of that pronoun, and both refer to the same
occupation. Type-1 items need the meaning of the sentence to resolve the
pronoun, and type-2 items can be resolved from syntax. The type-1 test split is
used for two probes, and both test splits are used for the antecedent test of
Section~\ref{sec:capmethod}. Sentence understanding after fine-tuning is
measured on two GLUE tasks \citep{wang2018glue}: SST-2 sentiment classification
and MRPC paraphrase detection.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Four encoders are trained on identical text and compared at checkpoints where
their validation loss matches. Each benchmark item then contributes one paired
difference between two encoders.

\subsection{Architectures}

The four encoders differ only in how often they reuse a transformer block, with
counts in Table~\ref{tab:arch}. All four share the tokeniser, the masked language
modelling objective, an effective depth of 12 block applications and the
representation width. The shared ratio is the fraction of those 12 applications
that reuse parameters already applied at another depth.

\emph{VanillaBERT} follows the BERT encoder design, with 12 independent blocks in
sequence \citep{devlin2019bert}. \emph{LoopedBERT} has entry blocks, a core of
shared blocks applied several times in a row, and exit blocks. A learned vector
is added to the token states before each pass of the core, so the shared blocks
can tell the passes apart. \emph{ALBERTLoopedBERT} applies one shared block at
every depth, following the ALBERT sharing scheme \citep{lan2020albert}.
\emph{HyperloopBERT} keeps the looped layout of \emph{LoopedBERT} and carries
$n=4$ parallel copies of each token vector, called streams, through the loop
\citep{zeitoun2026hyperloop}. The streams are mixed at each depth by
%
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell+1)} = \mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}
                      + \mathbf{B}^{(\ell)} f\!\left(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}\right),
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ stacks the $n$ stream
states of width $d$ at depth $\ell$ and $f$ is the shared transformer block. The
learned matrix $\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ mixes the
streams, and $\mathbf{B}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ distributes the
block output across them. With a single stream the update reduces to the
ordinary residual connection. The streams are merged before the exit blocks.

\subsection{Matching and Scoring}

Seven target values of validation loss, from 5.0 down to 2.2, were fixed before
any bias was scored. They come from the loss curve of a short pilot run. The
highest target lies where training leaves its early flat region. The lowest lies
just above the loss the training budget was expected to reach. Validation loss is
measured at regular intervals. The first checkpoint whose loss falls below a
target is saved as the encoder's snapshot for that target. Encoders are compared
only at snapshots for the same target. Between two measurements the
loss can pass more than one target, and the same checkpoint then serves each of
them. The analysis therefore holds 21 distinct snapshots for the seed-42 runs,
between four and seven per encoder.

Each sentence $s$ is scored by its pseudo-log-likelihood, the average log
probability of its tokens when each is hidden in turn. For a set $\mathcal{I}$ of
scored positions,
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PLL}(s) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}
                  \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} \log P\!\left(t_i \mid s_{\setminus i}\right),
\end{equation}
%
where $s_{\setminus i}$ is $s$ with position $i$ masked and $P$ is the output of
the masked language modelling head. The stereotype effect of a pair is
$e = \mathrm{PLL}(s_{\text{more}}) - \mathrm{PLL}(s_{\text{less}})$, positive
when the encoder prefers the more stereotypical member.

The three scoring rules differ in the scored positions and the aggregation. The
full-sentence rule scores every token except the start and end markers and
averages them. The shared-token rule averages over the tokens the two members have in common.
It finds them from the common start and the common end of the pair.
The official rule is the released \emph{CrowS-Pairs} scorer
\citep{nangia2020crows}. It finds every shared span by sequence alignment, scores
those tokens and sums their log probabilities without averaging. The
re-implementation of the official rule reproduces the published score of
\texttt{bert-base-uncased}: 60.48 against the published 60.5. The shared-token
rule cannot align eight pairs that differ in several places, which leaves it
1500 pairs.

At one matched point, the contrast between \emph{VanillaBERT} and another encoder
is the mean item difference $d_i = e_i^{\text{VanillaBERT}} - e_i^{\text{other}}$. A positive contrast
indicates that \emph{VanillaBERT} records the larger stereotype effect. A paired
sign-flip permutation test checks whether the mean difference is zero, with the
Phipson--Smyth $p$-value estimate \citep{phipson2010permutation}. That estimate
keeps a permutation $p$-value above zero when a finite number of random sign
flips is drawn. Intervals are percentile intervals from resampling the benchmark
items, so they describe item sampling only. Holm's step-down procedure controls
the family-wise error rate over the three contrasts against the same baseline.

\emph{StereoSet} items are scored by filler. The stereotype effect of an item is
the score with the stereotypical filler minus the score with the
anti-stereotypical filler. The standard stereotype score (SS) is the share of
items where the stereotypical filler scores higher. The language-modelling score
(LMS) is the share where a meaningful filler beats the unrelated one
\citep{nadeem2021stereoset}.

\subsection{Capability Checks}
\label{sec:capmethod}

A stereotype effect means little if the encoder does not follow the sentence it
scores, so three checks measure sentence understanding directly. The first is a
masked-pronoun probe on \emph{WinoBias} type 1. The pronoun is replaced with a
mask, and the probe records whether the encoder puts more probability on
masculine or on feminine pronouns. The pro-stereotypical and anti-stereotypical
versions of an item give the same masked input. This probe therefore measures
which pronoun gender the encoder prefers, not which occupation the pronoun
refers to.

The second check is an antecedent test that measures pronoun resolution. It
follows the masked-substitution method of \citet{kocijan2019surprisingly}. The
pronoun is replaced by masks for each candidate occupation in turn, such as
``the developer'' and ``the designer''. The encoder scores each candidate by the
mean log probability of its tokens at the masked positions. An item counts as
resolved when the referred occupation scores higher. The masculine version of
each item is used, so the possessive ``his'' maps to ``the developer's''
without ambiguity. A first-mention rule, which always picks the occupation named
first, gives a reference rate for each split.

The third check fine-tunes each encoder on SST-2 and MRPC with a classification
layer on the first token. It reports accuracy on the GLUE validation sets.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All four encoders share the data, the objective and every training setting in
Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings} except one. \emph{HyperloopBERT} uses half the peak
learning rate, because its loss became non-finite at the common rate. Its
comparisons therefore combine the stream design with a change made for training
stability, and they are not an isolated test of the streams. The learning-rate
schedule is set for 7 billion tokens with linear decay after warmup.

The encoders reach the lowest common matched point after different amounts of
training, between 1.53 and 2.07 billion tokens. Matching on validation loss
therefore does not match the token count. \emph{HyperloopBERT} later diverged
to a non-finite loss, and every analysed snapshot comes before that point.

All four encoders were trained with seed 42. \emph{VanillaBERT} and
\emph{LoopedBERT} were trained a second time with seed 43, which changes the
initial weights and the masking of the training text. The second runs use the
same corpus pipeline, the same validation set and the same learning-rate
schedule. They stop once the lowest matched point is crossed, because no later
checkpoint enters the comparison. The pre-specified test is the contrast of each
block-reusing encoder against \emph{VanillaBERT} at the lowest-loss matched
point. The other matched points, the loss adjustment, the other scoring rules and
the second seed were analysed after that first result.

\subsection{Matched-point Comparison}

At the lowest-loss matched point, two block-reusing encoders record about one
third less stereotype effect than \emph{VanillaBERT} on \emph{CrowS-Pairs}
(Table~\ref{tab:band}). The \emph{LoopedBERT} and \emph{HyperloopBERT} contrasts
survive Holm correction, and the \emph{ALBERTLoopedBERT} contrast does not.
Read alone, this is the result that would support a claim that block reuse
lowers measured association. No direct \emph{HyperloopBERT}-versus-\emph{LoopedBERT}
test is part of this comparison, so the result cannot be credited to stream
mixing.

The validation-loss curves show why the comparison has to be matched on loss
(Figure~\ref{fig:training}). \emph{VanillaBERT} and \emph{LoopedBERT} follow
nearly the same curve, and \emph{ALBERTLoopedBERT} stays higher throughout.
\emph{HyperloopBERT} leaves the early flat region first but later diverges.
Equal token counts would therefore compare the encoders at different levels of
language-modelling quality.

The masked-pronoun probes show the same kind of association in single
sentences (Table~\ref{tab:qual}). The probe ``The scientist said that
\texttt{[MASK]} was ready for the lab'' is repeated with ``teacher'' and
``secretary''. On the scientist sentence \emph{VanillaBERT} prefers \emph{he},
and on the teacher sentence it prefers \emph{she}. Bold marks the probes where
\emph{VanillaBERT}'s preferred pronoun matches the common occupational
stereotype. On the secretary sentence every encoder prefers \emph{he}, against
the stereotype. Pronoun frequency can therefore outweigh an occupational
association, which is why both benchmarks compare paired sentences that change
only the group term.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}
\label{sec:robust}

A robustness check repeats a finding under analysis choices that could
reasonably have been made differently. A finding that holds under only one set of
choices belongs to the analysis rather than to the design. Five choices are
varied: the matched point, the treatment of the remaining loss gap, the scoring
rule, the benchmark and the training seed.

The gap at the lowest-loss point does not hold at the other matched points
(Figure~\ref{fig:delta}). \emph{VanillaBERT} minus \emph{LoopedBERT} is negative
at all four less-trained distinct comparisons, so \emph{LoopedBERT} scores
higher there. At target 3.0 the opposite gap is itself significant, with loss
matched more closely than at the lowest point (Table~\ref{tab:seed}). Over the
seven matched points, the sign of the comparison depends on where the encoders
are compared.

Actual validation loss still differs slightly between snapshots that share a
target. A regression over all seed-42 snapshots therefore models the effect from
encoder design and actual loss together (Table~\ref{tab:model}). Actual loss
predicts the measured effect, and no design term is distinguishable from zero.
The remaining loss differences can thus account for the gaps that matching
leaves.

The scoring rule changes which contrasts reach significance
(Table~\ref{tab:rules2}). Under the shared-token rule and under the official
rule, \emph{VanillaBERT} still records the larger effect in every contrast.
None of those six contrasts survives Holm correction. The two significant
contrasts therefore depend on the full-sentence rule.

\emph{StereoSet} agrees on direction and disagrees on significance
(Table~\ref{tab:stereoset}). Every block-reusing encoder again records a lower
paired effect than \emph{VanillaBERT}. After correction only the
\emph{HyperloopBERT} contrast is significant, whereas \emph{CrowS-Pairs} singles
out \emph{LoopedBERT} and \emph{HyperloopBERT}. The two benchmarks share the
direction of the effect but not the set of encoders that differ.

A second seed tests whether the gap belongs to the design or to one training
run. \emph{VanillaBERT} and \emph{LoopedBERT} were retrained from a different
random initialisation and compared at the same matched points
(Table~\ref{tab:seed}). At the lowest-loss point the seed-43 gap is small and
not significant. At three of the four less-trained points, \emph{VanillaBERT}
now records the larger effect. The same design also differs between its own
runs. \emph{VanillaBERT} seed 42 minus seed 43 is 0.018 at the lowest-loss point
($p=0.002$), close to the seed-42 architecture gap. The official rule again
finds no significant seed-43 contrast. The sign and the significance of the
contrast therefore depend on the training run.

\subsection{Capability Checks}
\label{sec:capability}

The masked-pronoun probe shows no occupational pronoun preference on
\emph{WinoBias} type 1 (Table~\ref{tab:capability}). The pronoun-preference
column is the share of items where the encoder's preferred pronoun gender
matches the occupational stereotype. Every encoder run lies between 0.489 and
0.503, close to the value of one half that no preference would give. The
pro-stereotypical and anti-stereotypical versions give the same masked input, so
these values carry no information about pronoun resolution.

Zero-shot pronoun resolution is near chance for every encoder and for the public
\texttt{bert-base-uncased} model. On type 1 the antecedent accuracy of the six
encoder runs lies between 0.486 and 0.522, against 0.544 for
\texttt{bert-base-uncased}. On type 2 every model stays near or below one half,
although the referred occupation is almost always the one named second.
Zero-shot antecedent scoring therefore cannot separate an encoder that follows
the sentence from one that does not.

Fine-tuning shows that every encoder can use sentence content. After three epochs
per task, all six encoder runs reach between 0.865 and 0.883 accuracy on
\emph{SST-2} sentiment classification. Accuracy on \emph{MRPC} paraphrase
detection spreads more widely, from 0.686 to 0.762, on a small validation set.
On \emph{SST-2}, the two \emph{VanillaBERT} runs differ by as much as the
seed-42 designs do. The near-chance antecedent scores therefore reflect
zero-shot scoring rather than an inability to process sentences.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

A single matched comparison of measured association is a weak basis for an
architecture claim. In this study it gave a clear, corrected result in favour of
block reuse. The neighbouring matched points, the loss adjustment and the
benchmark's own scorer did not support that result. The measured effect follows
how far an encoder has trained, and block reuse changes how far an encoder
trains on a given budget. Design and training progress therefore remain
entangled at any one checkpoint, even after matching on validation loss.

The second seed weakens the architecture claim further. Its significant contrasts
fall at less-trained points, where the first seed found a smaller difference of
opposite sign. Variation between two runs of one design matched the gap between
designs at the lowest-loss point. A difference measured on one run per design
therefore mixes the design with the initialisation. Two runs remain few, so this
result shows instability rather than an estimate of seed variance.

Developers who select encoders for retrieval or classification find no support
here for preferring a block-reusing design on stereotype grounds. A comparison
meant to support such a choice needs several matched training levels, more than
one training run and more than one scoring rule. It also needs a capability test
that the encoders can pass, which at this scale means a fine-tuned task.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

The results cover four English encoders of at most 110 million parameters.
\emph{ALBERTLoopedBERT} and \emph{HyperloopBERT} were trained once, and
\emph{VanillaBERT} and \emph{LoopedBERT} twice. Only the lowest-loss contrasts
were specified in advance, and the other analyses are exploratory.
\emph{HyperloopBERT} used a lower learning rate, so its comparison is not an
isolated test of the streams. Both benchmarks are association instruments scored
from the same prediction head, and both carry documented item-quality problems
\citep{blodgett2021salmon}. The study does not measure systems built on these
encoders. An India-centric instrument covering caste and religion
\citep{khandelwal2023indianbhed} would extend the evaluation beyond English.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study tested whether a lower stereotype score can be credited to block
reuse once training progress is held level. Four encoders that differ only in
block reuse were trained on identical text and compared at matched validation
loss. At the most-trained matched point, two block-reusing encoders scored
significantly lower on \emph{CrowS-Pairs}. That gap reversed at less-trained
points and disappeared after adjustment for the remaining loss differences. The
official scoring rule and a second training run did not reproduce it. Two runs
of the same design differed nearly as much as the two designs. Zero-shot pronoun
resolution was near chance for every model, while fine-tuning showed that all
encoders process sentences. At this scale, block reuse therefore has no
demonstrated effect on stereotype association. Future comparisons can report
several matched training levels, several training runs and the official scoring
rule. Larger encoders and an India-centric benchmark would test whether the
instability persists beyond this setting. Code, per-item scores for every snapshot, the masked-position
predictions behind Table~\ref{tab:qual} and the analysis scripts are available at
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/HyperloopBert/}.
