\title{Same Models, Opposite Conclusions: Testing the Robustness of
       Architecture Bias Comparisons}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{abstract}
Retrieval systems rank documents, and text classifiers assign labels to documents. Many such systems reuse language encoders, which map text into token vectors whose values depend on surrounding text. Before developers select an encoder, stereotype benchmarks can test whether an encoder gives higher probability to text that links a social group with a stereotype. We compare four encoders on the same text. \emph{VanillaBERT} is an unshared baseline. \emph{LoopedBERT}, \emph{ALBERTLoopedBERT}, and \emph{HyperloopBERT} reuse block parameters across depth to different degrees. \emph{HyperloopBERT} also uses parallel streams, which are separate copies of token vectors mixed during computation. We evaluate three datasets: \emph{CrowS-Pairs}, a paired-sentence stereotype benchmark; \emph{StereoSet}, a sentence-completion stereotype benchmark; and \emph{WinoBias}, a pronoun-resolution capability check. We apply four sensitivity checks. At the lowest-loss matched point on \emph{CrowS-Pairs}, where models are compared after reaching the same held-out-text loss target, the full-sentence rule gives lower mean paired item differences for \emph{LoopedBERT} and \emph{HyperloopBERT} than for \emph{VanillaBERT}. The full-sentence rule scores every ordinary token in each sentence. Holm correction, a multiple-testing correction across contrasts against the baseline, keeps those contrasts significant. \emph{HyperloopBERT} also required a lower learning rate, so its comparison mixes stream design with a training-stability change. A regression adjusting for validation loss and checks with other scoring rules do not preserve the \emph{CrowS-Pairs} contrasts. On \emph{StereoSet} intrasentence items, only the \emph{HyperloopBERT} paired filler-effect contrast stays significant after Holm correction. The \emph{WinoBias} pre-set language-understanding check stays near chance, so this unfine-tuned masked-head probe cannot support downstream model-selection claims.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro-bundle}

Information retrieval systems rank documents for user queries, and text
classifiers assign labels to documents. Developers often choose a pre-trained
language encoder before building a retrieval or classification system. A
language encoder maps a text sequence into token vectors whose values depend on
the surrounding text. A stereotype benchmark measures whether a model gives
higher scores to text that links a social group with a stereotype. Before
task-specific fine-tuning, encoders can assign different scores to the same
stereotype benchmark items. These pre-training scores do not measure how a later
ranker or classifier behaves in deployment.

The measurement setting comes from masked language models and association
benchmarks. A masked language model is an encoder trained to predict tokens
hidden from their surrounding words. A social stereotype association is a higher
model score for text that links a social group with a common stereotype.
\emph{CrowS-Pairs} is a paired-sentence stereotype benchmark, and
\emph{StereoSet} is a sentence-completion stereotype benchmark
\citep{nangia2020crows,nadeem2021stereoset}. Both benchmarks estimate
associations by comparing model scores for paired texts.

One \emph{CrowS-Pairs} item makes the task concrete before the architecture is
introduced. The item contrasts ``\emph{Cheryl} was bad at saving money and thus
had a financial crisis'' with the same sentence using ``\emph{John}'',
Table~\ref{tab:data}. The task asks which sentence receives the higher model
score. The full-sentence rule scores each ordinary token while excluding the
special start and end markers. Under that rule, each scored token is hidden in
turn. The model probability of the original token is recorded. The log
probabilities are averaged for each sentence. The anti-stereotypical score is
subtracted from the stereotypical score. A positive item score means that the
encoder gives the stereotypical sentence the higher probability.

No prior study cited here compares weight-reusing encoders at matched
validation loss on these stereotype benchmarks. Model capability here means
language-modelling quality, measured by validation loss on held-out text.
Validation loss is the average negative log probability on held-out text. Lower
values indicate better language modelling. A checkpoint is a saved model state
at a training step. Lower validation loss can change the paired score difference
on benchmark items. A fixed training budget therefore mixes architecture with
training progress.

This study holds the tokeniser, the training text, the representation width and
the effective depth fixed, and compares encoders only where they have reached
the same validation loss. It then tests whether a lower measured association can
still be attributed to weight reuse. Weight reuse means that the same block parameters
are applied at more than one depth in the network. Effective depth is the number
of transformer-block applications made to each token. A transformer block is the
repeated attention-and-feed-forward unit used in BERT, the original
bidirectional transformer encoder design \citep{devlin2019bert}. A matched point
is a checkpoint selected by crossing the same validation-loss target. A contrast
is the mean paired difference between \emph{VanillaBERT} and another encoder on
the same benchmark items. Holm correction is a step-down procedure for several
contrasts against one baseline. Holm correction controls the family-wise error
rate.

The study treats an architecture comparison as stable only under stated
conditions. The contrast must keep the same sign at all matched points and under
all scoring rules. It must also keep the same sign under both direction
conventions. The two direction conventions either treat \texttt{sent\_more}, the
dataset field holding one sentence member, as stereotypical or use the dataset
direction label. A positive contrast means that the unshared baseline has the
larger stereotype score. A negative contrast means that the other encoder has
the larger stereotype score. These tests examine architecture, training
progress, scorer convention, and pronoun-resolution capability. They do not
isolate stream design from the lower learning rate used by \emph{HyperloopBERT}.

The method varies encoder design while holding the tokeniser, objective,
training text, representation width, and effective depth fixed. A tokeniser is
the fixed procedure that splits text into model tokens. Representation width is
the size of each token vector inside the encoder. The study compares the
encoders in Table~\ref{tab:arch}. \emph{VanillaBERT} is the unshared baseline
with independent transformer blocks. \emph{LoopedBERT} reuses a small core of
blocks across depth. ALBERT is a BERT variant that shares encoder-block
parameters across layers \citep{lan2020albert}. \emph{ALBERTLoopedBERT} reuses
one block across all block positions, following ALBERT. \emph{HyperloopBERT}
keeps the looped design and adds parallel representation streams mixed by
learned weights \citep{zhu2025hyperconnections}. A stream is one parallel copy
of token representations inside that model.

This paper makes three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item At the matched point where the encoders are furthest trained,
      \emph{LoopedBERT} and \emph{HyperloopBERT} give lower mean item scores on
      \emph{CrowS-Pairs} than the baseline does, and both contrasts survive Holm
      correction,
      Table~\ref{tab:band}. Both differences disappear once validation loss is
      adjusted for across all matched points, Table~\ref{tab:model}, and again
      under either alternative scoring rule, Table~\ref{tab:rules2}.
\item On \emph{StereoSet} the direction agrees, but only one contrast stays
      significant, and for a different encoder. On \emph{WinoBias} all four
      encoders answer at chance, Table~\ref{tab:gate}, which limits what any of
      these measurements can show about later use.
\item An architecture comparison should be reported across several matched
      points and more than one scoring rule before a difference is credited to
      the design.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:method} defines the encoders, the matching protocol, and the

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Prior work provides the stereotype measurements, the weight-reusing
architectures, and the reason to control capability when comparing encoders.

\emph{CrowS-Pairs} and \emph{StereoSet} compare model scores for stereotypical
and anti-stereotypical text \citep{nangia2020crows,nadeem2021stereoset}. A
pseudo-log-likelihood is the average log probability assigned to original tokens
when each token is masked in turn. Masked-position probing means scoring hidden
positions to measure an association. Both benchmarks build on masked-position
probing of social associations \citep{kurita2019measuring}.
\citet{blodgett2021salmon} found unclear or inconsistent items in both
benchmarks, and \citet{wang2025difference} distinguish association benchmarks
from tests of unequal treatment. The benchmarks used here are association
instruments. The present study uses the same instruments to ask whether an
architecture comparison changes under alternative scoring choices.

A separate literature changes encoder architecture by reusing weights across
depth. ALBERT ties one encoder block across all layers to reduce storage
\citep{lan2020albert}. \citet{saunshi2025looped} show that a block repeated in a
loop can approach the reasoning performance of a deeper unshared model.
\citet{bae2025mor} learn a per-token recursion depth over a shared block.
\citet{geiping2025huginn}, \citet{zhu2025ouro}, and \citet{frey2026adaptive}
study related recurrent-depth models. A residual connection adds a block input
to the block output before the next layer. Hyper-connections generalise that
connection to several parallel streams with learned mixing
\citep{zhu2025hyperconnections}, and \citet{xie2025mhc} constrain that mixing.
\citet{zeitoun2026hyperloop} combine looping with hyper-connections. The looped
and hyper-connection studies cited above mainly report language quality,
throughput, parameter efficiency, and reasoning accuracy. They do not test
social-bias measurements for these designs.

Capacity and compression studies are the closest precedent. Prior work shows
that pruning and other compression methods can change fairness measurements
\citep{hooker2020characterising,xu2022compression,ramesh2023comparative}.
\citet{voria2026tracing} localise stereotype-carrying neurons inside
pre-trained transformers. The present study varies weight sharing before
training and compares models at matched validation loss.

Fairness in information access is an established concern for retrieval and
ranking systems \citep{ekstrand2022fairness,singh2018fairness}. A claim that
one encoder design carries less stereotype association than another could affect
model selection. This study tests that claim for weight-reusing encoders under
matched-loss and scoring-rule checks. The measurements reported here do not
support that model-selection claim.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

One corpus trains the encoders, and the evaluation sets measure association or
capability. The concrete examples in Table~\ref{tab:data} make the tasks
visible, and the exact data choices are listed in Table~\ref{tab:datastats}.

All encoders in Table~\ref{tab:arch} are pre-trained on \emph{FineWeb-Edu}, a
filtered subset of web text selected for educational content
\citep{penedo2024fineweb}. The shared corpus size and sequence length are given
in Table~\ref{tab:datastats}. Each encoder sees the same text in the same order
under the same tokeniser. Therefore no difference between encoders can originate
in the training text or token segmentation.

The evaluation uses stereotype benchmarks and a capability check,
Table~\ref{tab:datastats}. \emph{CrowS-Pairs} and \emph{StereoSet} measure
association. The encoders are scored on these benchmarks without fine-tuning. A
consistent preference for the sentence or completion matching a common
stereotype is treated as evidence of lexical association.

\emph{CrowS-Pairs} contains paired sentences labelled by bias category and by
direction, Table~\ref{tab:datastats}. In the example data, \texttt{race-color}
names the race and colour category. The field \texttt{sent\_more} stores one
member of the sentence pair. The direction label says whether
\texttt{sent\_more} is stereotypical or anti-stereotypical. The direction label
affects the official scorer and is examined in the scorer analysis in the
Results section. Shared tokens are tokens that occur in both members of a
sentence pair. An unaligned shared-token pair is a pair for which the
shared-token alignment cannot recover the common token sequence. Category sizes
are uneven, so the Results section reports category behaviour rather than
relying only on the aggregate.

\emph{StereoSet} is used in its intrasentence split. In this split, each item
supplies a context sentence with a blank and candidate fillers. The fillers are
stereotypical, anti-stereotypical, or unrelated. The unrelated filler tests
whether the encoder prefers a meaningful completion over an unrelated one. The
split sizes are reported in Table~\ref{tab:datastats}.

\emph{WinoBias} is a pronoun-resolution dataset used here as a capability check
rather than a bias measure \citep{zhao2018winobias}. Each item contains
occupation terms and a pronoun that refers to exactly one occupation. Resolving
the pronoun requires sentence understanding. An encoder near chance on this task
cannot support a claim about stereotypical pronoun resolution. The split count
and items per split are in Table~\ref{tab:datastats}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

The method links the architecture comparison to item-level measurements. The
computation proceeds from encoder definition, to checkpoint matching, to
benchmark scoring, and then to the capability gate.

\subsection{Architectures}

Architecture is the design choice tested in the study, so all non-architectural
inputs are held fixed except the \emph{HyperloopBERT} peak learning rate,
Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings}. The compared encoders are ordered by weight reuse
and parameter count in Table~\ref{tab:arch}.

\emph{VanillaBERT} is the unshared control. It follows the BERT encoder design
with independent blocks in sequence \citep{devlin2019bert}. \emph{LoopedBERT}
uses entry blocks, a reused core, and exit blocks. Entry blocks are the
non-reused blocks before the looped core. Exit blocks are the non-reused blocks
after the looped core. The core blocks are applied several times in a row. A
learned depth marker is a learned vector added to token states before each pass.
The marker lets the reused core distinguish passes. \emph{ALBERTLoopedBERT}
uses one shared block throughout, following the ALBERT sharing scheme
\citep{lan2020albert}. \emph{HyperloopBERT} keeps the looped layout and carries
parallel streams through the loop. It combines the streams before each core
pass, writes the output back to every stream, and merges the streams before the
exit blocks.

All encoders use the same tokeniser, masked language modelling objective,
effective depth, and representation width. Their parameter counts and shared
ratios are shown in Table~\ref{tab:arch}. The shared ratio is the fraction of
effective-depth block applications that reuse block parameters.
\emph{HyperloopBERT} differs from the plain looped design by adding stream
mixing and by requiring a lower peak learning rate for stable training,
Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings}. That encoder is therefore an architecture bundle,
meaning a design that changes more than one mechanism at the same time.

The hyper-connected model carries parallel streams and mixes them at each depth.
The update is
%
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell+1)} = \mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}
                      + \mathbf{B}^{(\ell)} f\!\left(\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}\right),
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ stacks the stream states
at depth $\ell$. The symbol $n$ is the configured stream count, and $d$ is the
shared representation width. The paper does not tabulate those configuration
fields, so exact reruns require the released model configuration. The function
$f$ is the shared transformer block. The matrix $\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}$ mixes the
streams. The matrix $\mathbf{B}^{(\ell)}$ distributes the block output back
across streams. Both matrices are in $\mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. The
single-stream special case recovers the ordinary residual connection.

Relative to the hyper-connected looped transformer of
\citet{zeitoun2026hyperloop}, \emph{HyperloopBERT} shares the loop-and-stream
structure and the learned depth-wise mixing. It differs in being trained from
scratch at encoder scale under a masked language modelling objective. The
\emph{HyperloopBERT} results in this paper therefore cover only the
encoder-scale masked-language-model comparison on \emph{CrowS-Pairs} and
\emph{StereoSet} at the matched checkpoints analysed here. They do not cover
every possible hyper-connected looped model.

\subsection{Matching, scoring, and inference}
\label{sec:rules}

A list of target validation-loss values is fixed before training. The exact
target count and range are in Table~\ref{tab:runfacts}. Validation loss is
measured at regular intervals. A band is a pre-set target-loss level whose
crossing selects a snapshot. The first checkpoint below a target becomes the
snapshot for that band. Encoders are compared only at snapshots assigned to the
same band.

This matching is approximate. Several nominal bands can be crossed inside one
validation interval, so several bands can share a checkpoint. The analysis
therefore uses distinct snapshots, with the per-architecture counts shown in
Table~\ref{tab:runfacts}. Realised losses can also differ slightly inside a
band. The loss-adjusted Results analysis tests whether conclusions remain after
continuous adjustment for realised validation loss.

The sentence score is pseudo-log-likelihood. In each scoring rule, each selected
token position is masked in turn. The probability of the original token is then
evaluated, and the log probabilities are combined. Scoring is performed in the
precision reported in Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings}. Writing $\mathcal{I}$ for
the set of positions a rule scores,
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PLL}(s) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}
                  \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} \log P\!\left(t_i \mid s_{\setminus i}\right),
\end{equation}
%
where $s_{\setminus i}$ is the sentence with position $i$ masked and $P$ is the
masked language modelling head output. The per-item score is the difference
%
\begin{equation}
e = \mathrm{PLL}(s_{\text{stereo}}) - \mathrm{PLL}(s_{\text{anti}}) .
\end{equation}
%
A positive value means that the stereotypical member receives the higher score.

The token \texttt{[MASK]} is the placeholder inserted at a hidden position. The
\emph{full-sentence} rule scores every position except the special boundary
tokens \texttt{[CLS]} and \texttt{[SEP]}. The token \texttt{[CLS]} marks the
start of the sequence, and \texttt{[SEP]} marks the end or separation boundary.
The \emph{shared-token} rule scores only tokens that the two sentences have in
common. The shared-token alignment matches the common prefix and common suffix
of the two token sequences. Under this rule, demographic terms are not scored
but remain visible as conditioning context. The benchmark's released
implementation defines the \emph{official} rule. It selects token spans that the
released alignment matches in both sentence members. It sums log probabilities
over those spans and assigns the stereotypical role from the direction label.
Table~\ref{tab:rules} summarises these rules.

One \emph{CrowS-Pairs} item illustrates the difference. The dataset item pairs
``\ldots{}and whether he would come forward'' with ``\ldots{}and whether she
would come forward''. In that item, \texttt{sent\_more} is the
anti-stereotypical sentence and the label is \texttt{antistereo}. The
full-sentence and shared-token rules treat \texttt{sent\_more} as
stereotypical. The official rule reverses the role for that item because of the
direction label. The same pair can therefore contribute with the opposite sign
under the official rule.

The contrast is paired by benchmark item. Every item is scored under the
baseline and another encoder at the same matched point. The paired difference is
$d_i = e_i^{\text{base}} - e_i^{\text{other}}$. The contrast $\Delta$ is the
mean of those differences over the benchmark items. A positive contrast means
that \emph{VanillaBERT} records the larger stereotype effect.

The null hypothesis $\mathbb{E}[d_i] = 0$ is tested by a paired sign-flip
permutation test. The draw count and bootstrap count are in
Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings}. The $p$-value uses the Phipson--Smyth estimate
\citep{phipson2010permutation}. That estimate avoids a zero permutation
$p$-value when a finite number of random sign flips is sampled. Confidence
intervals come from resampling benchmark items with replacement. The reported
limits are percentile cutoffs from those resamples, so the intervals describe
item sampling only. Where several contrasts are formed against the same
baseline, Holm's step-down procedure controls the family-wise error rate within
that family.

\label{sec:gate}
The capability gate is a pre-set set of checks for basic language-understanding
ability. GLUE is a general language understanding benchmark used in the planned
gate \citep{wang2018glue}. The gate required above-chance performance on GLUE, a
baseline stereotype-effect interval excluding zero, and above-chance accuracy on
the pro-stereotype split of \emph{WinoBias}. All three conditions had to hold
before the study would make downstream model-selection claims. The
\emph{WinoBias} component tests whether the encoder resolves the pronoun at all.

For \emph{WinoBias}, the pronoun token is replaced with \texttt{[MASK]}. The
masked language modelling head scores the candidate pronouns from the item. A
prediction is correct when the higher-scoring pronoun matches the gold referent
encoded by the dataset item. Accuracy is the share of correct items in each
split.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The experimental setup fixes training data and most hyper-parameters across
architectures. A flat-loss region is an early training phase where validation
loss stays nearly constant. \emph{HyperloopBERT} needed a lower peak learning
rate because its loss became non-finite at the common rate,
Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings}. Table~\ref{tab:runfacts} gives the run-level
facts that affect interpretation, and Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings} gives
implementation settings.

The encoders reach the lowest-loss common matched point after different amounts
of training, Table~\ref{tab:runfacts}. Matching on validation loss therefore
does not match the training budget. \emph{HyperloopBERT} is the only architecture
with a different peak learning rate, Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings}. At the
common rate its loss became non-finite. Holding the rate fixed would have
compared stable models with a failed run. The reported \emph{HyperloopBERT}
comparisons therefore treat that model as a stability-adjusted architecture
bundle. They are not an isolated test of streams. Each encoder was trained once,
Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings}. Reported intervals quantify benchmark-item
uncertainty, not variation across training runs. The results use these matched
snapshots and report how the comparison changes with the analysis choice.

\label{sec:cont}
Matched-point variation leaves open whether realised validation loss explains
part of the single-point result. The regression uses \emph{CrowS-Pairs} item
effects as the response, architecture as a categorical factor with
\emph{VanillaBERT} as reference, and mean-centred realised loss as a covariate.
The model is fitted by least squares. Architecture and loss vary only across
snapshots, so the identifying units are snapshots rather than item rows. A wild
cluster bootstrap is an inference check over snapshots. It resamples
snapshot-level residual patterns while preserving dependence among item rows
from the same snapshot. Table~\ref{tab:model} reports the snapshot-level fit and
that bootstrap.

After adjustment for realised validation loss, no architecture coefficient in
the \emph{CrowS-Pairs} item-effect regression is distinguishable from zero under
the table decision rule, Table~\ref{tab:model}. Bold marks terms for which both
the snapshot and bootstrap $p$-values are below the chosen five-percent alpha,
Table~\ref{tab:model}. Realised loss remains associated with the mean
\emph{CrowS-Pairs} effect, Table~\ref{tab:model}. The realised-loss coefficient
in Table~\ref{tab:model} has a magnitude comparable to the single-point
contrasts in Table~\ref{tab:band}. Remaining validation-loss differences can
therefore change the interpretation of the lowest-loss matched comparison. The
absence of a detectable architecture coefficient is not evidence that
architecture has no effect. With the snapshot count in Table~\ref{tab:runfacts}
and the seed count in Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings}, the analysis has limited
power for small architecture effects. This is an absence of evidence at this
sample size rather than evidence of absence.

\label{sec:scorer}
The loss-adjusted regression still uses the full-sentence scorer. The
shared-token and official \emph{CrowS-Pairs} rules rescore the same items at the
lowest-loss matched snapshots. Table~\ref{tab:rules2} gives the exact contrasts
and the count of contrasts that survive Holm correction. Neither alternative
rule preserves the corrected result. Under the shared-token rule, no contrast
survives Holm correction. Under the official rule, no contrast survives
correction, and the \emph{LoopedBERT} contrast reverses sign,
Table~\ref{tab:rules2}.

The official rule differs from the shared-token rule in token alignment,
aggregation, and direction convention. The decomposition in
Table~\ref{tab:rules2} fixes the official token set and separates aggregation
from direction. Changing from averaging to summing changes scale. Changing the
direction convention changes sign for the \emph{LoopedBERT} and
\emph{ALBERTLoopedBERT} contrasts. The sign reversal is therefore attributable
to the benchmark direction convention for the \texttt{antistereo} subset, whose
size is given in Table~\ref{tab:datastats}. In Table~\ref{tab:rules2},
``Sig.'' counts contrasts that survive Holm correction at the chosen
five-percent alpha.

The released BERT checkpoint \texttt{bert-base-uncased} is the public base BERT
model trained on lowercased text by the original BERT authors. The checkpoint
was rescored with the official \emph{CrowS-Pairs} rule for the aggregate
stereotype score. That score differs from the value reported by
\citet{nangia2020crows}. The public checkpoint and current scoring library are
not the same execution environment as the original benchmark release. All
encoders in this paper are scored with the same code and the same environment,
so the architecture contrasts use a common scorer. The shared-token
prefix-and-suffix alignment matches the common prefix and suffix of the two
sentences. That alignment fails on the unaligned subset shown in
Table~\ref{tab:datastats}. These pairs are instances of the item-quality problem
documented by \citet{blodgett2021salmon}.

\label{sec:capability}
The capability gate limits the interpretation of all preceding effects. On
\emph{WinoBias}, accuracies for every encoder and split lie just above and just
below chance, Table~\ref{tab:gate}. An encoder that cannot resolve the pronoun
cannot support a claim about stereotypical preference in pronoun resolution. The
GLUE component was not run because the failed pronoun-resolution component
already made the gate fail. The baseline-interval component is met by the
\emph{VanillaBERT} row of Table~\ref{tab:band}.

The failed \emph{WinoBias} check limits the reported effects to lexical
association claims from the unfine-tuned masked language modelling head. The
reported effects do not show that a ranker or classifier fine-tuned from
\emph{VanillaBERT}, \emph{LoopedBERT}, \emph{ALBERTLoopedBERT}, or
\emph{HyperloopBERT} would treat demographic groups differently in use. These

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The corrected contrast holds at one matched \emph{CrowS-Pairs} point and does
not hold under the other checks. At the lowest-loss \emph{CrowS-Pairs} point,
\emph{LoopedBERT} and the \emph{HyperloopBERT} bundle are lower than
\emph{VanillaBERT}, Table~\ref{tab:band}. That corrected contrast does not
repeat across matched points or alternative scorers, Figure~\ref{fig:delta} and
Table~\ref{tab:rules2}. All encoders used the same data order, scorer code, and
snapshot-selection rule. The remaining issue is dependence on the matched point
and scorer.

Remaining differences in realised validation loss are associated with the
\emph{CrowS-Pairs} item effect. After continuous adjustment for realised
validation loss, no architecture term is distinguishable from zero at the stated
decision rule, Table~\ref{tab:model}. Validation loss remains associated with
the \emph{CrowS-Pairs} item effect, Table~\ref{tab:model}. This association is
not a causal proof. The snapshot count is limited, and all checkpoints come from
the seed count reported in Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings}.

The scorer dependence comes from rule choices. The three scoring rules make
different choices about token sets, aggregation, and direction labels,
Table~\ref{tab:rules}. The decomposition shows that the sign reversal follows
from the direction convention for \texttt{antistereo} pairs,
Table~\ref{tab:rules2}. Treating \texttt{sent\_more} as stereotypical and using
the dataset direction label are both explicit conventions. A conclusion that
holds under only one convention is therefore not enough in this study to
attribute the measured difference to architecture alone.

Developers of retrieval and classification systems often select among
pre-trained encoders. In this study, an architecture-level association
comparison changes under matched capability level, scoring rule, and direction
convention. The failed capability gate and single-seed design also limit the
claim. The comparison therefore does not support model-selection claims in this
study.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Several limits affect how the results should be read. Training uses a single
seed per architecture, Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings}. No run is repeated. Every
interval reported here bootstraps over benchmark items and quantifies item
sampling alone. The intervals do not quantify variation between training runs.
The ordering of encoders may change under re-training with another seed. The
single-seed design also means that the loss trajectories in
Figure~\ref{fig:training} cannot separate architecture from run-specific
optimisation variation.

The analyses across matched points, continuous loss adjustment, scoring-rule
decomposition, and category comparisons were performed after the single-point
result had been examined. The analyses are exploratory. Only the lowest-loss
point contrasts against \emph{VanillaBERT} were specified in advance. The
reported corrected results should therefore be read as measurements from this
run, not as confirmatory evidence for a general architecture effect.

The encoders have not converged at the lowest-loss matched point. The validation
losses in Table~\ref{tab:band} are still changing, and Figure~\ref{fig:training}
does not show a finished training curve. Whether the architecture contrasts
would differ for fully trained encoders is untested here. \emph{HyperloopBERT}
also needed a lower peak learning rate, Table~\ref{tab:reprosettings}. Its
comparison therefore bundles the stream architecture with a stability
adjustment.

Only two stereotype benchmarks are used, Table~\ref{tab:datastats}. Both are
correlation-type instruments in the sense of \citet{wang2025difference}. Both
are scored from the same masked language modelling head. Agreement between these
instruments is therefore weaker evidence than agreement between independent
measurement approaches would be. The capability gate fails,
Table~\ref{tab:gate}, so no statement about downstream behaviour is supported.
Both instruments also have documented item-quality problems
\citep{blodgett2021salmon}. The unaligned \emph{CrowS-Pairs} subset in
Table~\ref{tab:datastats} is one instance.

The scoring-rule decomposition isolates the direction convention at one matched
point. The decomposition does not prove that the same attribution holds at other
matched points or on \emph{StereoSet}. Those settings were not rescored under
the official rule. The category analysis is also descriptive. Category sizes are
uneven, Table~\ref{tab:datastats}, and no category hypothesis was specified
before examining the aggregate.

The evaluation is English-only. An India-centric instrument covering caste and
religion \citep{khandelwal2023indianbhed} was planned, but the available hosted
copy of the dataset could not be used with the English-only tokeniser. Extending
the protocol to that setting is the first follow-up direction.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The study began from a practical question about encoder choice. If one
weight-reusing encoder appears to have lower stereotype association than an
unshared encoder, the comparison should remain similar under nearby matched
training points and stated scoring rules.

This study compared encoders that differ in weight reuse across depth, while
holding data, tokenisation, objective, representation width, and effective depth
fixed. At the lowest-loss matched point on \emph{CrowS-Pairs}, \emph{LoopedBERT}
and the \emph{HyperloopBERT} bundle show lower effects than \emph{VanillaBERT}.
Both contrasts survive Holm correction, Table~\ref{tab:band}. That result does
not hold across the subsequent checks. The looped result appears at only one
matched point, Figure~\ref{fig:delta}. The architecture terms become
indistinguishable from zero after continuous loss adjustment,
Table~\ref{tab:model}. Under both alternative scoring rules, no contrast stays
significant after correction, Table~\ref{tab:rules2}. The official rule reverses
the \emph{LoopedBERT} and \emph{ALBERTLoopedBERT} contrasts because of the
direction convention for \texttt{antistereo} pairs.

The second benchmark gives a related but different result. On \emph{StereoSet}
intrasentence items, the direction agrees for all weight-reusing encoders. Only
the \emph{HyperloopBERT} paired filler-effect contrast stays significant after
Holm adjustment, Table~\ref{tab:stereoset}. The category analysis and capability
gate further limit the interpretation, Figure~\ref{fig:robust} and
Table~\ref{tab:gate}. Bias comparisons should report matched capability levels,
scoring rules, and direction conventions before attributing a measured
difference to encoder design.
